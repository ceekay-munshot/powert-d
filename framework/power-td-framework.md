Power T&D investment framework
For any Power T&D company, the first question should not be “is T&D a good theme?” The real question is:
Where exactly does this company sit in the grid capex cycle, and does that position translate into durable earnings, cash flow and ROCE?
Power T&D is the grid layer between generation and the consumer. Transmission is high-voltage movement over long distances; distribution is lower-voltage last-mile delivery. InVed breaks the value chain into transmission lines, substations, transformers, switchgear / circuit breakers on the transmission side, and distribution transformers, feeder networks, metering and billing systems on the distribution side.
The sector backdrop is strong: InVed cites FY22–27 transmission capex requirement of ₹4.25 lakh crore, with planned transformation capacity addition of 7,76,330 MVA, versus 3,49,685 MVA added during FY17–22. For FY27–32, it cites 76,787 ckm transmission lines, 4,97,855 MVA transformation capacity and 32,250 MW HVDC bi-pole capacity, requiring ~₹4.91 lakh crore capex.
But don’t stop at TAM. Ask: what part of this TAM can the company actually win?

1. Identify the exact business bucket first
Do not club all T&D companies together. Their economics are very different.

| Bucket | Examples of business type | Core diligence question |
| Transmission asset owner | PGCIL, Adani Energy, IndiGrid | Is regulated / bid-based asset growth compounding at attractive ROE? |
| Transformer manufacturer | Hitachi, GE Vernova, CG Power, Atlanta, TARIL, Voltamp | Is the company moving up the voltage/value chain? |
| HVDC / FACTS / grid-stability OEM | Hitachi, GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, BHEL, Quality Power | Does it have technology capability in complex grid projects? |
| Transmission EPC | KEC, Kalpataru, Transrail, Skipper, Bajel, Techno Electric | Can it execute without working-capital blow-up? |
| Towers / conductors / cables | Skipper, Apar, KEC, Transrail, Diamond Power, Polycab, KEI | Is it commodity manufacturing or differentiated T&D exposure? |
| Distribution / DISCOM | Tata Power, CESC, Torrent Power | Can it reduce AT&C losses and grow regulated equity? |
| Smart meters / grid automation | HPL, Genus, Schneider, Techno Electric, others | Is the revenue annuity-like or one-time execution? |
| Component suppliers | CRGO cores, laminations, bushings, radiators, OLTC, insulators | Are they bottleneck suppliers or low-margin vendors? |


2. Sector demand: is the company exposed to the right capex pool?
Start with where demand is coming from.

| Area | Questions to ask |
| Transmission capex | Is demand coming from new interstate transmission, intrastate transmission, RE evacuation, data centres, industrial load, railway electrification, or replacement demand? |
| Voltage mix | Is demand shifting from 132/220 kV to 400/765 kV? Is the company eligible for that shift? |
| Line vs substation | Is the opportunity more in ckm transmission lines or MVA/GVA transformation capacity? Which part does the company benefit from? |
| Timing | Are tenders already awarded, under bidding, under NCT approval, or only in long-term NEP plans? |
| Customer | Is the customer PGCIL, state utilities, private RE developers, industrials, data centres, export OEMs, or EPC contractors? |

Why this matters: a company exposed to 765 kV transformers / HVDC / GIS / STATCOM is not the same as one exposed to 11–33 kV distribution transformers.

3. Product positioning: is the company in a high-value or commodity part of T&D?
For transformer / equipment companies:

| Diligence area | Key questions |
| Voltage class | What is the highest voltage class supplied: 33 kV, 66 kV, 132 kV, 220 kV, 400 kV, 765 kV, or HVDC converter transformers? |
| MVA capability | What is the maximum MVA rating? Has the company executed at that level or only received approval? |
| Product mix | Power transformers, distribution transformers, furnace transformers, rectifier transformers, inverter duty transformers, reactors, traction transformers? |
| Approval depth | Approved by PGCIL? NTPC? SEBs? Private RE developers? Export utilities? |
| Testing infrastructure | Does it have in-house testing for EHV/UHV? Or does it depend on third-party labs? |
| Failure history | Any transformer failures, warranty claims, blacklisting, delayed type tests? |
| Technology | Parent technology access? JV? Own design capability? Licensed technology? |

Good sign: company is moving from lower-voltage distribution transformers to 220 kV / 400 kV / 765 kV / HVDC.
Bad sign: order book is growing, but mostly in low-voltage commodity products where margins can mean-revert quickly.
Atlanta RHP explains the voltage hierarchy well: EHV includes 765 kV, 400 kV and 220 kV; high voltage includes 132 kV and 66 kV; medium voltage includes 33 kV, 11 kV, 6.6 kV and 3.3 kV. It also notes that higher-voltage transformers above 220 kV are used for long-distance transmission, RE integration and grid-stability projects.

4. HVDC / grid-stability optionality: does the company have scarce capability?
This is one of the biggest differentiators.

| Area | Questions |
| HVDC capability | Can the company supply HVDC converter transformers, valves, reactors, control systems or only peripheral equipment? |
| Technology type | Is it exposed to LCC HVDC, VSC HVDC, or both? |
| Qualified supplier pool | Is it one of the few qualified players? |
| Order wins | Has it actually won HVDC projects or is it just “eligible”? |
| Grid stability | Does it supply STATCOM, FACTS, shunt reactors, capacitor banks, synchronous condensers? |
| Parent dependency | If parent technology is needed, what economics stay in India? |

MOSL notes that HVDC projects require specialized high-value converter transformers and reactors, with a limited supplier pool including Hitachi Energy India, Siemens Energy India, GE Vernova T&D India and BHEL. It also highlights a 32.3 GW HVDC pipeline, of which 14.5 GW had already been tendered/awarded, and expects 1–2 HVDC awards annually going forward.
Investment implication:
HVDC capability can justify premium multiples because it is not easy for every transformer company to enter. But you must verify whether the Indian listed entity captures the profit pool or only executes low-value manufacturing.

5. Order book quality: not all order books are equal
Order book is the most abused metric in capital goods.

| Diligence area | Questions |
| Order book size | Order book / trailing revenue? Is it 1x, 2x, 3x, 5x? |
| Order composition | Transmission, distribution, exports, industrials, renewables, data centres, railways? |
| Voltage mix | How much is 400/765 kV vs lower voltage? |
| Margin quality | Are new orders at higher/lower margins than old orders? |
| Price escalation | Is copper, aluminium, steel, CRGO pass-through available? |
| Fixed-price exposure | What portion is fixed price? What happens if copper/CRGO rises? |
| Execution period | 6 months, 12 months, 24 months, 36 months? |
| Customer concentration | Top 5 customers as % of order book? |
| Geography | Domestic vs export; state concentration; high-risk geographies? |
| Cancellation risk | Any LoA but no PPA / no financial closure / no land / no ROW? |

Good order book: high-voltage, pass-through contracts, credible customers, manageable execution period, low receivable risk.
Bad order book: impressive headline size but fixed-price, state-heavy, low-margin, high working-capital, delayed site readiness.

6. Manufacturing capacity and bottlenecks
For equipment makers, don’t just ask “capacity kitni hai?” Ask usable capacity at the right voltage class.

| Area | Questions |
| Installed capacity | Capacity in MVA/GVA, MT, bays, units? |
| Usable capacity | How much capacity is actually usable for 400/765 kV or only for lower kV? |
| Capacity utilization | Current utilization? Peak utilization? Bottleneck process? |
| Expansion | What capacity is being added, at what capex, and by when? |
| Asset turns | Revenue per GVA / revenue per ₹ of gross block? |
| Lead time | Manufacturing lead time for low-voltage vs high-voltage transformers? |
| Testing bottleneck | Is testing capacity limiting dispatches? |
| Skilled manpower | Are design engineers / winding specialists / testing engineers available? |
| Backward integration | Does it make tanks, radiators, cores, laminations, bushings, or outsource? |

MOSL says transformer demand remains strong from domestic and export markets, while supply has struggled to keep pace, causing longer lead times. It says leading players like Hitachi, GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, CG Power, Atlanta and TARIL are adding around 200–220 GVA capacity over 2–3 years.
Key question:
Will capacity expansion increase margins because the company moves up the value chain, or will it simply add commodity capacity and create future oversupply?

7. Supply chain: where can margins get hurt?
For transformers and T&D equipment, input cost and supply availability matter a lot.

| Input / component | Why it matters |
| CRGO steel | Core raw material for transformers; import dependence and price volatility matter |
| Copper / aluminium | Major cost item; pass-through terms are critical |
| Transformer oil | Cost and availability matter |
| Bushings | Can be a bottleneck, especially high-voltage bushings |
| OLTC | Critical component; quality failures can be costly |
| Radiators / tanks | Fabrication bottleneck can delay dispatch |
| Semiconductors / electronics | Relevant for HVDC, automation, relays, digital grid systems |
| Freight / logistics | Large transformers are logistically difficult; export freight can hurt margins |

MOSL lists key risks for transmission equipment players as semiconductor unavailability, supply-chain disruption, tendering slowdown, BESS scaling challenges and copper price increases.
Simple test:
Ask management: “For your latest order book, what % of raw material is covered by pass-through, what % is hedged, and what % is exposed?”

8. Execution capability and working capital
This is especially important for EPC and project-heavy companies.

| Area | Questions |
| ROW risk | Who is responsible for right-of-way: client or EPC contractor? |
| Land / forest clearance | Are approvals in place before execution starts? |
| Payment milestones | Advance, dispatch payment, erection payment, commissioning payment, retention? |
| Receivables | Debtor days by customer: PGCIL, SEB, private, exports? |
| Retention money | What % of revenue is stuck as retention? |
| Bank guarantees | BG requirement as % of order value? |
| Subcontracting | What is outsourced vs done in-house? |
| Cost overrun | Who bears commodity, labour, freight and delay costs? |
| Litigation | Arbitration claims? LD claims? Disputed receivables? |
| Cash conversion | EBITDA vs operating cash flow over 3–5 years? |

InVed’s EPC comparison shows why working capital is central: players differ sharply on working-capital days, EBITDA margins and ROCE. For example, it flags KEC as having 57% export order book and being the largest tower manufacturer, while also showing EPC working-capital intensity varying meaningfully across companies.
OPTL’s RHP also highlights classic EPC risks: delays in statutory approvals, credit constraints for smaller EPC firms, climate/weather disruption, import dependency on critical equipment, and technology/IP risks.
Rule of thumb:
In EPC, high revenue growth without cash flow is dangerous. Always reconcile order book → revenue → EBITDA → CFO → FCF.

9. Customer quality and payment risk

| Customer type | What to check |
| PGCIL / central utilities | Better payment quality, higher approval standards |
| State transmission utilities | More variable payment cycles; state-specific risk |
| DISCOMs | Higher receivable risk unless payment security exists |
| Private RE developers | Check financial closure, PPA status, evacuation connectivity |
| Industrial / data centre customers | Better payment but project timing risk |
| Export customers / parent entities | Good for margins, but check related-party pricing |

For utilities, Citi says the risk has shifted from broad DISCOM insolvency to more selective behaviour, with SECI and central structures still acting as important buffers.
Key diligence point:
A company with 40% growth but weak customers may deserve a lower multiple than a slower-growing company selling to PGCIL/export OEMs.

10. Domestic vs export opportunity
Exports are a meaningful upside trigger, but they need proper diligence.

| Area | Questions |
| Export share | Exports as % of revenue and order book? |
| Product exported | Low-voltage commodity transformers or high-voltage equipment? |
| End customer | Direct utility, global OEM, parent entity, EPC contractor? |
| Certifications | Does it meet IEC/IEEE/UL/ANSI standards? |
| Margin | Are export margins higher or just volume-filling? |
| Currency | Natural hedge or forex risk? |
| Repeatability | One-off order or recurring approved-supplier relationship? |
| Parent channel | For MNCs, how much export opportunity accrues to Indian listed entity? |

MOSL argues export opportunities are opening because global transformer demand is strong due to RE integration, data centres, industrial electrification, EV charging and replacement of ageing infrastructure. It notes transformer lead times in US/Europe have extended to 2–4 years, and that the US imports around 80% of power transformers and 50% of distribution transformers.
Good sign: repeat export orders from global utilities / parent networks.
Red flag: export revenue jumps because of one-off low-margin order.

11. Margin sustainability
Do not extrapolate peak margins blindly.

| Area | Questions |
| Mix benefit | Are margins rising because of higher voltage / exports / HVDC / service mix? |
| Pricing power | Is the company actually raising prices or just benefiting from tight supply? |
| Cost pass-through | How much raw material volatility is passed on? |
| Operating leverage | What is fixed cost absorption at current utilization? |
| Outsourcing | Are margins inflated due to outsourcing/low capex model, or at risk when scaling? |
| Warranty | Are warranty provisions adequate? |
| Normalized margin | What was margin across previous downcycle? |
| Competition | Will new capacity compress margins after FY27/FY28? |

MOSL is positive but also warns that valuations are no longer cheap and that the cycle has already driven sharp growth in order books, revenue and margins since FY22–23.
Investor trap: Buying a transformer company at peak margins, assuming current EBITDA margin is the new base.

12. ROCE and cash conversion
A good T&D company should not just show PAT growth. It should show cash-backed ROCE.

| Metric | Why it matters |
| Order book / revenue | Visibility |
| Book-to-bill | Order momentum |
| EBITDA margin by segment | Mix quality |
| Gross margin | Pricing power and input pressure |
| Working capital days | Cash conversion |
| Receivable days | Customer/payment quality |
| Inventory days | Capacity bottlenecks / raw material stocking |
| CFO / EBITDA | Earnings quality |
| FCF after capex | Real cash generation |
| ROCE / ROIC | Capital efficiency |
| Revenue / gross block | Asset turns |
| Net debt / EBITDA | Balance-sheet risk |
| Warranty provision / sales | Product quality risk |

Most important test:
If revenue and EBITDA are growing but CFO is weak for 2–3 years, the company may be funding customers rather than earning real returns.

13. Transmission asset-owner framework
For transmission asset owners, the framework is different from equipment companies.

| Area | Questions |
| Regulated vs TBCB | How much is cost-plus regulated tariff vs tariff-based competitive bidding? |
| Regulated equity | Is regulated equity growing? |
| Capitalization | Is capex converting into commissioned assets on time? |
| CWIP | Is CWIP building up without capitalization? |
| ROE | What is allowed ROE? Any sharing of gains? |
| Cost of debt | Funding advantage vs private players? |
| Project pipeline | Won projects, under bidding, identified pipeline? |
| ROW | Right-of-way delay risk? |
| Commodity exposure | In TBCB projects, who bears input-cost inflation? |
| Dividend vs growth | Is cash being reinvested or paid out? |

Citi’s PGCIL risk section is a useful checklist: it flags right-of-way as the most important near-term execution risk, weaker-than-expected new awards, higher competitive intensity, supply-chain tightness in transformers/reactors/GIS/HVDC, and input-cost pressure under TBCB projects.

14. Distribution / DISCOM framework
For distribution companies, the question is not transformer demand. It is loss reduction + regulated equity growth + collection efficiency.

| Area | Questions |
| AT&C losses | Current losses and glide path? |
| Collection efficiency | Is cash collection improving? |
| Capex plan | How much regulated equity gets added? |
| Tariff orders | Are tariffs cost-reflective? |
| Regulatory assets | Any under-recovery building up? |
| Smart meters | How many installed? Impact on billing and theft reduction? |
| Feeder monitoring | Is the network digitized? |
| Outage metrics | SAIDI / SAIFI improvement? |
| Privatization optionality | Can the company win more DISCOMs? |

Citi calls T&D the anchor of Tata Power’s story, with Mumbai/Delhi offering annuity-like stability and Odisha adding a self-help growth leg. It notes Odisha AT&C losses fell to 18.5% in 9MFY26 from 20.4% a year earlier, helped by network upgrades, digital metering and operational restructuring.

15. Transformer manufacturer framework
Core investment question
Is this company merely a transformer manufacturer, or is it moving up the value chain into higher-voltage, higher-MVA, higher-margin, more approval-led products?
This bucket needs the deepest diligence because “transformer” can mean anything from a low-voltage distribution transformer to a 765 kV / HVDC converter transformer. Economics are very different.
MOSL notes that the T&D cycle is especially strong for high-voltage transformers, driven by India’s ~₹9 trillion transmission capex plan till FY32, but also warns that valuations are no longer cheap and earnings upgrades / exports are needed to sustain multiples.
A. Product and voltage-class diligence

| Area | Questions to ask | Why it matters |
| Voltage class | What is the current revenue split across 11/33/66/132/220/400/765 kV? | Higher voltage = higher entry barrier, fewer suppliers, better pricing power. |
| MVA capability | What is the maximum MVA transformer actually supplied, not just designed or approved? | Execution history matters more than claimed capability. |
| Product mix | Power transformers, distribution transformers, furnace transformers, rectifier transformers, inverter duty transformers, generator transformers, reactors, traction transformers? | Special-purpose transformers usually have better margins than plain vanilla distribution transformers. |
| High-voltage approval | Is the company approved by PGCIL, NTPC, state TRANSCOs, private utilities, railways, export utilities? | Vendor approval is a moat. Without it, TAM is theoretical. |
| Voltage migration | Is the company moving from 33/66/132 kV to 220/400/765 kV? | This is one of the biggest rerating triggers. |
| Renewable exposure | Does it supply inverter duty transformers / pooling substation transformers / step-up transformers for solar and wind? | RE evacuation is a structural demand driver. |
| Data centre / industrial exposure | Does it supply to data centres, metals, railways, metro, EV charging, green hydrogen? | These are non-utility demand pools and may carry better payment terms. |

Atlanta’s RHP highlights that transformer demand is being driven by RE integration, thermal/hydro capacity, government schemes, EV charging, railways, data centres and smart-grid development. It also notes that advanced transformers need smart-grid technologies, phase-shifting capabilities and voltage regulation features.
B. Manufacturing capability diligence

| Area | Questions |
| Installed capacity | Capacity in MVA/GVA — but split by voltage class. How much is actually usable for 220 kV+? |
| Capacity utilization | Is utilization 60%, 80%, 100%? Where is the bottleneck? |
| Expansion plan | What capacity is being added, at what capex, by when, and for which voltage class? |
| Testing infrastructure | Does it have impulse testing, partial discharge testing, temperature-rise testing, short-circuit testing capability? |
| In-house vs outsourced | Does it manufacture tanks, radiators, cores, windings, insulation kits, bushings, OLTCs in-house? |
| Design capability | In-house design team or dependent on consultants / technology partners? |
| Skilled labour | Any shortage of winding engineers, testing engineers, design engineers? |
| Warranty history | Transformer failure rate? Warranty claims? Product recalls? Blacklisting? |

Transformer manufacturing is not just “assembly”. The bottleneck is often testing, design, skilled labour, CRGO availability, high-voltage bushings and quality control. Atlanta’s RHP explicitly calls out quality/reliability, compliance with standards, technology upgradation, price sensitivity, long lead times and working-capital management as key challenges for transformer manufacturers.
C. Raw material / margin diligence

| Input | Questions to ask |
| CRGO | What % is imported? From which countries? Any supply agreement? Inventory days? |
| Copper / aluminium | Pass-through available or fixed-price exposure? |
| Insulation material | Domestic sourcing or imported? |
| Transformer oil | Is price volatility passed through? |
| Bushings | In-house / domestic / imported? Any lead-time bottleneck? |
| OLTC | Supplier concentration? |
| Radiators / tanks | In-house fabrication or outsourced? |
| Freight | Who bears logistics cost for large transformers? |

The key margin question:
For the current order book, what % has price escalation for CRGO, copper, aluminium, freight and forex?
If raw material pass-through is weak, current margins may not be sustainable.
D. Order-book quality diligence

| Area | Good order book | Weak order book |
| Voltage mix | 220/400/765 kV, reactors, generator transformers, special transformers | Mostly 11/33/66 kV commodity transformers |
| Customer | PGCIL, NTPC, export utilities, high-quality private customers | State utilities with payment delays |
| Pricing | Escalation clauses, advance payments | Fixed price, long execution |
| Execution | 6–18 months with manageable lead times | Long-dated, low visibility, site delays |
| Margin | New orders at same/better margin than current | Order book growth but falling gross margin |
| Repeatability | Repeat customers, approved vendor status | One-off orders |

E. Key KPIs

| KPI | What to monitor |
| Order book / revenue | Visibility |
| Book-to-bill | Momentum |
| Revenue by voltage class | Mix improvement |
| Gross margin | Pricing power and RM pass-through |
| EBITDA margin | Operating leverage |
| Working capital days | Cash conversion |
| Inventory days | CRGO/copper stocking |
| Warranty provision / sales | Product quality |
| Revenue / GVA capacity | Asset productivity |
| ROCE / ROIC | Quality of growth |
| CFO / EBITDA | Earnings quality |

F. Red flags

| Red flag | Interpretation |
| High growth but no voltage-wise disclosure | Mix may not be improving. |
| “Approved up to 400 kV” but no actual executed orders | Capability is not yet monetized. |
| Rising order book + falling gross margin | Company may be buying orders. |
| Receivables rising faster than revenue | Customer/payment risk. |
| Large working-capital debt | Transformer growth can become balance-sheet heavy. |
| Warranty claims rising | Quality risk; can destroy reputation. |
| Capacity expansion only in lower voltage | May not deserve premium valuation. |


16. HVDC / FACTS / grid-stability OEM framework
Core investment question
Does the company have scarce grid-stability technology, or is it only a regular equipment supplier riding the same T&D cycle?
This bucket deserves premium diligence because India’s grid is becoming more complex. More solar/wind means more intermittency, voltage instability, reactive power issues and long-distance power transfer. That creates demand for HVDC, STATCOM, SVC, shunt reactors, series capacitors, FACTS, digital substations and protection systems.
A. Technology-position diligence

| Area | Questions |
| HVDC role | Does the company supply converter transformers, valves, control systems, thyristors/IGBTs, reactors, filters, protection systems, or only civil/electrical balance of plant? |
| LCC vs VSC | Is it capable in line-commutated converter HVDC, voltage-source converter HVDC, or both? |
| FACTS exposure | STATCOM, SVC, TCSC, UPFC, series capacitors, shunt reactors? |
| Reactive compensation | Capacitor banks, reactors, harmonic filters, power-quality equipment? |
| Grid automation | SCADA, relays, control panels, digital substations, PMUs, asset performance management? |
| Parent technology | For MNCs, what technology comes from parent and what economics stay in the Indian listed entity? |
| Domestic manufacturing | Is India only an assembly location or a real manufacturing/export hub? |
| Qualification | Is it qualified for PGCIL / HVDC / 765 kV / GIS / STATCOM tenders? |

InVed lists the major grid-stability devices: shunt capacitor banks, series capacitors, shunt reactors, series reactors, SVC, STATCOM, TCSC, UPFC, harmonic filters, power-quality monitors and surge protection devices. It also maps listed suppliers such as BHEL, Siemens, GE T&D, Hitachi Energy, CG Power, Schneider and HPL across these systems.
B. Project and order-quality diligence

| Area | Questions |
| Order type | Is it product sale, turnkey substation, HVDC terminal, STATCOM package, service/spares? |
| Margin structure | Are HVDC/FACTS margins higher than base transformers/switchgear? |
| Imported content | How much is imported from parent/global network? |
| Execution duration | HVDC can be multi-year; how much revenue conversion annually? |
| Risk-sharing | Who bears technology failure, liquidated damages, commissioning delays? |
| Service annuity | Is there long-term O&M, spares, software, digital service revenue? |
| Export opportunity | Can the India entity export grid equipment into parent network? |

GE Vernova T&D’s MOSL section is useful as a model: its product offering spans power transmission equipment, grid automation, grid systems integration, HVDC, STATCOM, FACTS, electrification software and smart-grid platforms.
C. KPIs to monitor

| KPI | Why important |
| HVDC/FACTS order inflow | Scarcity-value indicator |
| Share of high-voltage / digital / grid systems orders | Mix improvement |
| Service and spares revenue | Higher-quality recurring revenue |
| Imported content % | Determines margin capture in India |
| Gross margin by segment | Shows real technology premium |
| Execution timeline | Long-cycle revenue risk |
| LD provisions | Indicates project execution risk |
| R&D / engineering headcount | Depth of technology capability |
| Export order book | Global competitiveness |

D. Red flags

| Red flag | Meaning |
| Claims HVDC exposure but only supplies balance-of-plant | Lower technology moat. |
| High parent dependence but low margin capture | Indian listed entity may not get the full profit pool. |
| Large turnkey orders with low disclosure | Execution risk hidden. |
| Heavy imported content | Forex, supply chain and margin leakage. |
| No service/spares layer | One-time project business, not platform-like. |
| No actual STATCOM/HVDC wins | Theme exposure may be overstated. |


17. Towers / conductors / cables framework
Core investment question
Is this a commodity metal conversion business, or a structurally advantaged transmission-line supplier with scale, approvals, exports and integrated EPC pull-through?
This bucket is more cyclical and commodity-sensitive than transformer/HVDC OEMs. The moat comes from approvals, scale, logistics, galvanizing capacity, tower testing, export access, integrated EPC and working-capital discipline.
InVed’s component map breaks HV transmission lines into towers, conductors, OPGW/shield wires, insulators, hardware/fittings, vibration dampers, arcing horns, grounding accessories and safety markers. It lists tower players such as Skipper, KEC, Kalpataru, Jyoti Structures, Bajel and Transrail, and conductor/OPGW players such as Apar and Advait.
A. Product-position diligence

| Segment | Questions |
| Towers | Lattice towers, monopoles, tubular towers, multi-circuit towers? Up to what voltage — 220/400/765/800/1200 kV? |
| Conductors | ACSR, AAAC, AAC, HTLS conductors, aluminium alloy conductors? |
| OPGW | Does it supply optical ground wire for telecom + protection? |
| Cables | EHV, HT, LT, underground cables? Any 220 kV / 400 kV cable capability? |
| Hardware | Insulators, clamps, spacers, dampers, fittings? |
| EPC integration | Is manufacturing captive to EPC, or sold to third parties/export? |
| Testing | Tower testing station? NABL/ISO? Type-tested for UHV? |

B. Raw material and margin diligence

| Input | Diligence question |
| Steel | Is tower pricing pass-through or fixed? |
| Zinc | Galvanizing cost pass-through? |
| Aluminium | Critical for conductors; is LME pass-through available? |
| Power/fuel | Energy cost sensitivity? |
| Freight | Who bears freight, especially export? |
| Scrap | Is scrap recovery meaningful? |
| Forex | Export receivables hedged? |

Simple test:
For towers/conductors, gross margin can move sharply with steel, zinc and aluminium. Always ask: what % of order book has raw-material escalation clauses?
C. Capacity and manufacturing diligence

| Area | Questions |
| Tower capacity | MT per annum? Utilization? |
| Galvanizing | In-house galvanizing or outsourced? Bottleneck? |
| Conductor capacity | MT/km capacity? Product mix? |
| Testing facility | Can it test 765 kV / 1200 kV towers? |
| Export approvals | Approved by utilities in US, Europe, Middle East, Africa? |
| Logistics | Proximity to ports? Heavy structure transport capability? |
| Integration | Does EPC order book consume internal towers/conductors? |

D. Order-book quality

| Good order book | Weak order book |
| Export utilities / PGCIL / large EPCs | Low-margin state orders |
| High-voltage towers / HTLS / OPGW | Plain commodity towers |
| Pass-through contracts | Fixed-price metal exposure |
| Shorter execution with advance | Long execution with retention |
| Repeat customers | One-off geography/customer |

E. KPIs

| KPI | Why important |
| Order book in ₹ crore and MT | Visibility |
| Export order book % | Margin and diversification |
| Capacity utilization | Operating leverage |
| EBITDA / MT | Better than only EBITDA margin |
| Gross margin | Commodity pass-through |
| Working capital days | EPC + manufacturing risk |
| Inventory days | Metal stocking risk |
| ROCE | Asset efficiency |
| Debt / EBITDA | Cyclicality risk |

F. Red flags

| Red flag | Meaning |
| Order book growth but EBITDA/MT falling | Low-quality orders. |
| High export growth but rising receivables | Country/customer risk. |
| Fixed-price contracts with high metal volatility | Margin blow-up risk. |
| EPC losses hidden inside manufacturing | Integrated model may mask segment economics. |
| Very high inventory | Metal price speculation or slow-moving orders. |


18. Smart meters / grid automation framework
Core investment question
Is this company selling one-time hardware, or building an annuity-like digital grid platform?
Smart meters and grid automation are not the same. Smart meters are the customer-end device; grid automation includes SCADA, DMS, relays, sensors, PMUs, digital substations, outage tools and communication networks.
InVed’s T&D component table includes Smart Grid & SCADA/DMS, communication infrastructure, AMI and smart meters as distinct parts of the power-delivery chain.
A. Business model diligence

| Model | Questions |
| Meter manufacturing | Is it only selling smart meters as hardware? |
| AMI service provider | Is it responsible for installation, communication, data and maintenance? |
| DBFOOT / TOTEX model | Does it invest upfront and recover over years? |
| SaaS/software | Any recurring software, billing, analytics, outage management revenue? |
| EPC + O&M | Does it have installation and long-term O&M? |
| Utility digital platform | Does it provide SCADA, DMS, control-room software, asset management? |

The most important distinction:
Hardware sale = lower multiple.
Hardware + communication + software + long-term O&M = potentially better business.

19. Transmission EPC framework
Core investment question
Can this EPC company convert order book into cash, or will growth get consumed by working capital, claims and execution delays?
Transmission EPC is attractive because capex is large, but it is also the easiest place to lose money. Execution, ROW, working capital and claims matter more than headline order book.
OPTL’s RHP clearly splits transmission EPC into the supply portion — towers, conductors, conductor hardware, optical fibre cable and stringing accessories — and the service portion — route survey, design, engineering, excavation, tower foundation, erection, stringing, testing, commissioning and statutory approvals.

20. Component supplier framework
Core investment question
Is this component supplier a genuine bottleneck vendor with pricing power, or just a low-margin fabrication vendor to transformer / EPC companies?
This bucket includes CRGO laminations, transformer cores, windings, tanks, radiators, bushings, OLTCs, insulation material, transformer oil, CT/PT components, insulators, clamps, hardware, dampers, OPGW accessories and substation components.

Valuation questions

| Question | Why it matters |
| Are current margins above mid-cycle? | Avoid paying peak multiple on peak margin |
| Is order book growth already priced in? | Many T&D names have rerated |

| Is ROCE sustainable after expansion? | New capex can dilute ROCE initially |
| Does the company deserve premium to peers? | Only if voltage mix, technology, approvals or exports are superior |
| Is growth capex or working capital funded? | Equity dilution / debt risk |

MOSL explicitly says valuations are no longer cheap, but further earnings upgrades and export opportunities can sustain valuations.

11. Required management question list
Always provide 10–15 management questions, customized to the bucket.
Questions must be sharp and diligence-oriented.
Avoid generic questions like “What is your growth outlook?”
Examples:
“What is your revenue split by voltage class?” 
“What is the highest MVA rating actually executed, not just approved?” 
“What % of the order book has CRGO/copper pass-through?” 
“What is debtor days by PGCIL / state utility / private / export?” 
“How much of the order book is fixed price?” 
“What % of current capacity is usable for 400 kV and above?” 
“What is the warranty claim history over five years?” 
“What portion of HVDC economics is retained by the Indian listed entity?” 

12. Number discipline
Every number must have:
Source 
Date / period 
Unit 
Whether it is actual, estimate, guidance or assumption 
Use clear units:
ckm for transmission line length 
MVA/GVA for transformation capacity 
MW/GW for HVDC transfer capacity / generation capacity 
₹ crore / ₹ billion / ₹ trillion for capex / order book / revenue 
MT for towers / steel / conductors 
% for margins, ROCE, working capital ratios 
If a number is calculated, show the formula.

13. Treatment of missing data
If data is not available, say:
“Not disclosed in available company / project files.”
Then infer only if reasonable, and clearly mark:
Inference: based on product mix / peer comparison / customer profile.
Never fill gaps with made-up estimates.

14. Peer comparison discipline
Compare only with relevant peers.
Examples:
Transformer company vs transformer peers, not DISCOMs. 
EPC company vs EPC peers, not Hitachi/Siemens unless comparing project scope. 
Smart meter company vs AMI / metering peers. 
Transmission owner vs asset-owner / regulated utility peers. 
Peer comparison should include:
Product mix 
Voltage capability 
Order book 
Margins 
ROCE 
Working capital 
Valuation 
Balance sheet 
Export exposure 
Approval depth
